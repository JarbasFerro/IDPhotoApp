# Calipic — Color strategy research

**Status:** Research baseline; no color approved.  
**Reviewed:** 2026-09-17

## 1. Objective

Select a Calipic color system based on human perception, brand fit, accessibility, photographic neutrality, and platform behavior rather than on simplistic “color psychology” folklore.

The decision must support the approved brand personality:

- elegant;
- friendly;
- photographic;
- practical;
- sophisticated;

and avoid:

- complicated;
- AI-branded;
- generic;
- childish;
- cheap.

The product UI is intentionally extremely minimal and portrait-led, so the brand hue is expected to function mainly as a **selective accent and identity cue**, not as a large-area theme.

---

## 2. Research caution: color meaning is contextual

The strongest scientific conclusion is not “blue always means trust” or “red always means danger.” Color can affect perception and behavior, but context, saturation, brightness, culture, learned associations, and surrounding content all matter.

Elliot & Maier's review of color psychology concludes that color can carry meaning and influence affect/cognition/behavior, but the literature has important boundary conditions and is not mature enough to justify simplistic universal prescriptions.

A large cross-cultural study by Jonauskaite et al. tested 4,598 participants from 30 nations and found substantial commonality in color-emotion associations, while also finding meaningful country, linguistic, and geographic differences.

### Brand rule

> **Use color research to eliminate poor choices and frame hypotheses; use product/icon testing to choose the winner.**

Do not choose a hue from a generic chart alone.

Sources:

- Elliot, A. J. & Maier, M. A. (2014), *Color Psychology: Effects of Perceiving Color on Psychological Functioning in Humans*. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115035
- Jonauskaite, D. et al. (2020), *Universal Patterns in Color-Emotion Associations Are Further Shaped by Linguistic and Geographic Proximity*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620948810

## 3. Evidence from brand-perception research

Labrecque & Milne studied how logo color affects perceived brand personality. Their results are useful directional evidence, not a deterministic branding formula.

The most relevant reported associations include:

| Hue | Reported brand-personality tendency | Calipic initial interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Blue | Competence | Strong functional fit; major risk of generic tech/finance appearance |
| Black | Sophistication | Strong elegance fit; risk of coldness/severity and weak category warmth |
| Purple | Sophistication | Potentially distinctive; risk of feeling artificial/beauty/AI-coded depending execution |
| Pink | Sincerity / sophistication | Can feel human; high risk of narrowing audience or becoming beauty-coded |
| White | Sincerity | Excellent neutral foundation but not sufficiently ownable alone |
| Red | Excitement | Strong salience; risks urgency, error semantics, government/passport cues, and visual aggression |
| Orange | Excitement | Friendly and visible; risk of playful/cheap character conflicting with restraint |
| Yellow | Sincerity / excitement in some studies | Friendly/high visibility; risk of childishness, warning semantics, and weak light-mode contrast |
| Green | Less directly aligned with Calipic's target traits in the cited brand study | Can suggest nature/success; possible semantic conflict with “pass” state |
| Brown | Ruggedness | Poor fit with the intended photographic/sophisticated character |

Source:

- Labrecque, L. I. & Milne, G. R. (2012), *Exciting red and competent blue: the importance of color in marketing*. Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science 40, 711–727. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y

Important: hue is only one variable. Saturation and value materially change perceived personality. A highly saturated blue and a muted dark blue should not be treated as the same brand experience.

## 4. Calipic-specific evaluation of major color families

### 4.1 Blue

**Potential strengths**

- competence association is strongly aligned with an app dealing with exact output;
- native familiarity on iOS;
- works well in both light and dark appearances;
- generally easy to create accessible variants;
- can feel calm when moderately saturated.

**Risks**

- extremely common in software, finance, healthcare, security, and government services;
- may make Calipic look generic, violating one of the strongest anti-traits;
- system-blue proximity can erase the distinction between brand identity and default iOS interactivity.

**Conclusion:** viable only if the exact hue/geometry/icon combination is highly ownable. Avoid “default app blue.”

### 4.2 Cyan / teal

**Potential strengths**

- can retain some blue-family clarity while feeling more photographic/digital;
- can differentiate from default iOS blue;
- may feel clean and contemporary.

**Risks**

- saturated cyan easily becomes tech/AI/futuristic;
- teal can drift into wellness/health aesthetics;
- some blue-green combinations are difficult for certain color-vision deficiencies if relied upon semantically.

**Conclusion:** useful exploratory territory, especially in a darker/desaturated form, but must avoid “AI SaaS” styling.

### 4.3 Green

**Potential strengths**

- positive/success associations in many Western interfaces;
- can feel calm and friendly.

**Risks**

- Calipic already needs green-like semantics for pass/success if system conventions are followed;
- using green as the identity color can blur brand and validation meaning;
- nature/ecology associations are not relevant to the product proposition.

**Conclusion:** weak primary-brand candidate. Better retained for semantic success behavior through system colors.

### 4.4 Yellow

**Potential strengths**

- highly recognizable;
- can feel friendly and optimistic;
- strong App Store shelf differentiation when executed well.

**Risks**

- can feel playful or inexpensive, conflicting with sophisticated restraint;
- poor contrast against light backgrounds unless darkened considerably;
- overlaps with caution/warning semantics;
- large yellow surfaces can visually interfere with neutral portrait presentation.

**Conclusion:** interesting as a tiny identity accent or icon experiment, not an obvious UI accent winner.

### 4.5 Orange

**Potential strengths**

- human, approachable, energetic;
- highly visible;
- less generic than blue.

**Risks**

- excitement/energy is not the brand's main emotional goal;
- can become playful, promotional, or inexpensive;
- may compete too strongly with skin tones in portrait-heavy compositions.

**Conclusion:** possible but strategically weaker than cooler or more neutral directions.

### 4.6 Red

**Potential strengths**

- high recognition and strong visual ownership;
- can look sophisticated at darker values.

**Risks**

- strong urgency/error associations in interfaces;
- overlaps with fail/destructive states;
- higher visual arousal conflicts with the restrained brand posture;
- can evoke official/government document aesthetics in some executions.

**Conclusion:** do not prioritize as Calipic's primary UI accent. It would require exceptional evidence to overcome semantic conflict.

### 4.7 Purple / violet

**Potential strengths**

- empirical association with sophistication supports the brand personality;
- more ownable than blue;
- can work elegantly in both light and dark appearances.

**Risks**

- heavily used by generative-AI products, creative software, beauty, and “magic” functionality;
- Calipic explicitly rejects AI-branded character;
- high saturation quickly becomes expressive rather than restrained.

**Conclusion:** worth testing only in restrained, non-neon, non-gradient execution. Strong candidate if it can avoid AI-category cues.

### 4.8 Pink / magenta

**Potential strengths**

- can feel human, friendly, and sophisticated;
- highly ownable.

**Risks**

- can be read as beauty/retouching or gender-targeted, both undesirable for an ID-photo utility;
- bright magenta increasingly carries generative/creative-software cues.

**Conclusion:** low-priority primary candidate.

### 4.9 Black / graphite / monochrome

**Potential strengths**

- strongest direct fit with sophistication and visual restraint;
- lets portraits and photography dominate;
- naturally supports the “brand almost disappears” principle;
- can make a distinctive symbol depend on form rather than hue.

**Risks**

- pure black branding can become cold, luxury-coded, severe, or inaccessible when used indiscriminately;
- monochrome alone may not create enough App Store recognition;
- Dark Mode requires nuanced dynamic values rather than simply inverting black/white.

**Conclusion:** very strong **foundation** candidate, probably paired with one selective accent rather than used as the entire identity.

### 4.10 White / neutral light surfaces

**Potential strengths**

- sincerity/clarity associations;
- excellent photographic neutrality;
- naturally aligned with Apple's content-first visual language.

**Risks**

- not an identity by itself;
- cannot define Dark Mode;
- overuse can become sterile or generic.

**Conclusion:** use as a semantic system/background behavior, not the brand color itself.

## 5. Platform and accessibility constraints

Apple's Human Interface Guidelines support restrained brand color, but recommend system colors where possible and require custom colors to work across Light Mode, Dark Mode, and Increase Contrast.

Apple explicitly recommends:

- do not use color alone to communicate essential state;
- maintain sufficient contrast;
- supply appearance/contrast variants for custom colors;
- consider cultural interpretation;
- use color consistently rather than assigning one color multiple meanings;
- avoid excessive colored control backgrounds, especially with visually rich content.

This strongly reinforces Calipic's existing approach: the portrait is visually rich content, so navigation/toolbars should usually remain neutral and the brand color should be selective.

Sources:

- Apple HIG — Color: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color
- Apple HIG — Accessibility: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility
- Apple HIG — Dark Mode: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode

### Calipic implementation requirements

Any final custom accent must have:

- Light Mode value;
- Dark Mode value;
- Increase Contrast Light value;
- Increase Contrast Dark value;
- verified text/icon contrast where used;
- Display P3 and sRGB evaluation where relevant;
- color-blindness simulation/testing;
- physical-device testing under bright and dim ambient light.

Color can never be the only signal for pass/warn/fail/manual-check states.

## 6. Semantic-color conflict matrix

Calipic requires separate semantic states. The primary brand color must not cause those states to become ambiguous.

| Semantic role | Likely system family | Identity conflict risk |
|---|---|---|
| Pass / success | Green | High if brand is green |
| Warning / review | Yellow / orange | High if brand is yellow/orange |
| Fail / destructive | Red | High if brand is red |
| Information / link / action | Blue/system accent | Moderate-high if brand resembles default system blue |
| Manual check | Neutral / secondary symbol treatment | Lower |

This is a strong argument for exploring an identity accent that is **not** identical to the major traffic-light status families.

---

## 7. Photographic-neutrality rule

Because users must judge a face, background, edge quality, and final crop, large saturated brand surfaces around the portrait are undesirable regardless of hue.

The UI should normally use system-neutral backgrounds/materials around the image and reserve the brand accent for:

- primary action emphasis when appropriate;
- selected states where not semantically confusing;
- the app icon;
- brand mark;
- small guidance/crop details;
- marketing graphics;
- a very small number of signature moments.

This means the color decision can optimize for **identity and recognition** without forcing the entire editor to be tinted.

## 8. First candidate set for design experiments

No candidate is approved. The first visual prototypes should compare at least these four systems:

### Candidate A — Distinctive deep blue

A blue far enough from default iOS blue to feel deliberate, with moderate saturation and strong Light/Dark variants.

**Hypothesis:** competence + calm + native familiarity.  
**Main failure mode:** generic utility app.

### Candidate B — Restrained violet

A dark/desaturated violet with no neon gradients.

**Hypothesis:** sophistication + ownability.  
**Main failure mode:** AI/creative-software associations.

### Candidate C — Graphite + small cool accent

Almost monochrome UI/brand system with identity concentrated in the symbol and one cool accent.

**Hypothesis:** strongest photographic focus and sophisticated restraint.  
**Main failure mode:** insufficient App Store differentiation.

### Candidate D — Dark cyan / blue-teal

A controlled blue-green family clearly separated from system blue.

**Hypothesis:** photographic cleanliness + friendliness + modernity.  
**Main failure mode:** AI/wellness/SaaS feel.

Yellow/orange should be explored only as challenger icon concepts if the first set lacks warmth or shelf recognition.

---

## 9. Test protocol before color lock

For each candidate, create the **same** designs with only the color system changed:

1. app icon at full size;
2. app icon at realistic App Store/Home Screen size;
3. onboarding/home screen;
4. guided-camera overlay;
5. Photo Check pass/warn/fail state;
6. editor around varied skin tones and backgrounds;
7. print-sheet preview;
8. purchase-credit screen;
9. Light Mode;
10. Dark Mode;
11. Increase Contrast;
12. grayscale rendering.

Then evaluate:

- immediate ID-photo recognition;
- elegance;
- friendliness;
- sophistication;
- photographic neutrality;
- category differentiation;
- accessibility;
- consistency with native iOS;
- whether it looks “AI-powered” unintentionally;
- whether it appears cheap or childish.

Do not choose a hue until the symbol/icon geometry is tested with it. In Calipic, **form and color should be selected together**.

---

## 10. Current research conclusion

The evidence does **not** support selecting a color solely because it allegedly represents one emotion.

For Calipic, the strongest current direction is:

> **neutral photographic surfaces + one restrained, highly ownable accent.**

Blue is functionally aligned but dangerously generic. Violet is sophisticated but carries AI-category risk. Graphite/monochrome best protects photography and restraint but needs an accent for recognition. Dark cyan/teal is a useful challenger if it can avoid technology clichés.

The next step is visual prototyping, not additional abstract color symbolism.
